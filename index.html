Mission

About the program

GEVIP is a multi-year research program that brings together students across astronomy, physics, and related fields. Students learn astronomical data analysis, read scientific literature, develop a capstone proposal, and carry out novel research with mentorship from the PI, postdocs, graduate students, and senior undergraduates.

Senior project 01

Dust Through Time: Tracing Attenuation from Reionization to Cosmic Noon

Maddie Gerard

View details
My project aims to investigate how the shapes of dust attenuation curves relate to the physical properties of galaxies across cosmic time. I will measure how attenuation curves evolve with redshift in galaxies at z≈2-6, linking these variations to underlying dust and galaxy properties. To do this, I will combine uniform photometric catalogs from the UNICORN survey with NIRSpec/PRISM spectroscopy from the CAPERS program to study the evolution of dust attenuation. Using the SED–fitting code BAGPIPES, I will derive stellar masses, UV spectral slopes, and stellar attenuation curves. By comparing stellar and nebular attenuation at fixed stellar mass and across redshifts, this project will test predictions of dust evolution models and quantify how dust properties relate to galaxy age, star-formation history, and the UV continuum slope as an independent probe of dust geometry and reddening. The combination of uniform photometry, deep spectroscopy, and flexible SED modeling will provide a strong dataset for studying the buildup and transformation of dust during the peak of cosmic star formation.
Senior project 02

Studying Balmer line emission flux ratios in EMBER [working title]

Ananya Gupta

View details
Using the G395M filter in this EMBER dataset, find a sample of broad-line AGN. Study H-alpha/H-beta flux ratios of these AGN by comparing the broad regions of both the emission lines as well as the narrow regions, and seeing if those ratios are the same or not. We’d expect the ratio of the broad H-alpha/broad H-beta to be similar to the ratio of narrow H-alpha/narrow H-beta. If we see a difference in the ratios, that would be interesting, and we could start considering reasons why the object is facing reddening, and whether it’s systematic across the sample (be it dust, etc). Furthermore, depending on where we see the flux ratio difference (be it narrow or broad region), it would tell us if the object is obscured by dust evenly or if the galaxy/AGN is more affected, etc. [I'll give you a better version of this soon, but this is what I have right now]
Senior project 03

Narrow-line AGN Selection in Quiescent Galaxies: Exploring AGN Feedback in the Early Universe

Daisy Attaway

View details
Massive quiescent galaxies in the early universe are observed as early as 700 million years after the Big Bang, and the primary proposed mechanism of quenching these galaxies is feedback active galactic nuclei (AGN). However, the relationship between AGN and their host galaxies in the early universe remains unclear, especially for narrow-line AGN. I will identify candidate quiescent galaxies at 1 < z < 7 from the RUBIES and EMBER surveys using rest-frame UVJ colors. Using spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting, I will confirm the galaxies' quiescence and identify their star formation histories and possible AGN fraction. To further identify NLAGN, I will use emission line diagnostic diagrams. By exploring quenching timescales and searching for outflows within this sample, I will probe the relationship between NLAGN and their host galaxies.
Senior project 04

EMBER: Evaluating Emission-Line Diagnostic Diagrams for High-Redshift Narrow-Line AGN Selection

Kyra Sampson

View details
Although the James Webb Space Telescope has enabled the identification of activate galactic nuclei (AGN) in surprising numbers, narrow-line (type 2) AGN (NLAGN) are highly underrepresented in the current literature. However, traditional NLAGN identification methods break down at high redshift due to lower metallicities and higher ionization parameters, conflating active with star-forming galaxies. To address these issues, I sample NLAGN in the EMBER JWST survey at z > 2, where I apply a combination of traditional, recalibrated, and novel emission-line diagnostic diagrams in the optical and UV regimes to select a sample of NLAGN. I look to characterize the physical properties of this NLAGN sample and their host galaxies to better understand the role of AGN in the early Universe, as well as evaluate the validity of emission-line diagnostic diagrams as an identification tool in future work.
Senior project 05

Aging Galactic Giants in the COSMOS and CEERS Surveys: The Role of Halo Quenching in the Rapid Evolution of Massive Quiescent Candidates at z>3

Cyril John

View details
Massive Quiescent Galaxies (MQGs) at high redshifts have been a center of focus for JWST research because they require unusually rapid evolution to explain their growth and declining star formation before cosmic noon. More specifically, MQGs located in overdense cosmological regions which seed galaxy clusters offer insights into the role of galaxy interaction and dark matter structures in their quenching. My project aims to quantify how dark matter haloes and past merging (as evidenced by active galactic nuclei (AGN) activity) drive star formation and initiate quenching in these MQGs. Thus, I am using photometry from the COSMOS and CEERS JWST surveys to identify MQGs within protocluster environments using Baker et al. (2025) selection criteria and density mapping methods from Kakimoto et al. (2026). I am also using BAGPIPES to analyze their spectra from CAMPFIRE to determine whether these MQGs have synchronized quenching and exhibit strong AGN features. I aim to finally match observed MQGs star formation histories (SFHs) to simulated SFHS from halo growth histories using UniverseMachine catalogs. This work will expand the current understanding of MQG evolution mechanisms at high redshifts.
Senior project 06

The hunt for transients

Abby Kerrigan

View details
Supernovae (SNe) are indicators of stellar evolution and star forming activity within galaxies, and the connection between SNe type and a galaxy’s star formation rate (SFR) is essential in understanding how stellar populations evolve. With the goal of analyzing the distribution of SNe across host galaxies of varying star formation rate and metallicity, we detect and classify SNe within the Next Generation Deep Extragalactic Exploratory Survey (NGDEEP), using observations from JWST. My project focuses on using multi-epoch imaging to find changes in brightness between image timescales, isolating transient events such as SNe. After detecting transient candidates in NGDEEP, I will expand this pipeline to the JWST Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey (JADES). This work allows for classification of transients through light curve fitting, and an overall step forward in my goal of studying the relationship between the local population of SNe and host galaxy characteristics.

The program begins in sophomore spring and continues through senior year, progressing from introductory training to an independent capstone project.

    Complete introductory modules, begin a group starter project, and present results at the Undergraduate Research Forum (URF).

  2. 02
    Junior fall

    Capstone proposal

    Finish the group project. Develop and propose an individual capstone research project.

  3. 03
    Junior spring

    Capstone research

    Begin the capstone project and present preliminary results at the URF Symposium.

  4. 04
    Senior fall

    Analysis & results

    Continue the capstone project. Students pursuing graduate school are encouraged to present preliminary results at AAS.

  5. 05
    Senior spring

    Final presentation

    Complete the capstone project or final URF poster. Projects will result in an honors thesis, research note, or published paper.

Program information

Frequently asked
questions

Information about eligibility, coursework, time commitment, and research mentorship.

How is GEVIP structured?

Students who meet the prerequisites enroll in AST211G in the spring semester; no application is required. Sophomores work through training modules and a group starter project; during junior fall they read papers and develop an individual Capstone Project proposal; students then work on the Capstone until graduation.

Who can enroll in AST211G?

Prerequisites: sophomore standing; Astronomy 307 with a grade of at least B+; and Astronomy 376R, 275, or 375 with a grade of at least B+. Eligible students can enroll in AST211G in the spring semester.

Do I need coding experience?

Some Python experience and astronomical background are helpful, but not required. The first-semester coding modules are designed to bring members to a level where they can complete their first project and develop their own future project.

What data does GEVIP work with?

GEVIP works with a variety of datasets, including observations from JWST, Euclid, and HET.

How much time does research take?

GEVIP is a for-credit research group. Each credit hour corresponds to about three hours of work per week, and members attend at least two meetings each week.

What do students produce?

Members are expected to develop and conduct an individual Capstone Project and present at the Undergraduate Research Forum every spring. Students pursuing graduate school are encouraged to present at AAS in their senior fall; a Capstone can also lead to an AAS Research Note or a published paper.

What kind of mentorship is available?

Sophomores are paired with junior or senior mentors and a graduate student for their first project. Upperclassmen receive guidance from graduate students or postdocs on their Capstone, with regular Wednesday workshops for research problems and discussion.

What makes GEVIP different?

The vertically integrated structure gives undergraduates direct contact with graduate students, postdocs, the PI, and peers while they develop independent research interests and scientific communication skills.
